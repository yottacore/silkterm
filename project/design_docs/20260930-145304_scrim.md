## Summary

The text scrim is a soft backing in the background color that's drawn behind every glyph, so text stays readable over a busy wallpaper or a see-through window. A crisp outline of the same color can be drawn around the letters as well. Both are on by default.

## Specification

- Behind the terminal's text, SilkTerm draws a soft halo in the color of the background under each character. On a cell with its own background color, the halo takes that cell's color.

- The halo never covers the text, the cursor, or a link underline. It's drawn under all 3.

- The halo applies to terminal text only. The menu bar, the tab bar, and dialogs don't have one.

- Settings controls the halo with:
	- an on/off switch;
	- Strength, where each 20% doubles its opacity, up to five doublings at 100%;
	- Radius, how far it extends from the ink;
	- Softness, how gradually it fades;
	- Function, how its shape is worked out from the glyphs;
	- Falloff, the curve it fades along.

- A halo always ends at its radius, whatever the other settings are.

- The outline is separate from the halo. It works with the halo off. Its width is in pixels, and 0 turns it off. There isn't any performance profile that draws an outline wider than 1 pixel.

- The cursor can join the halo and the outline, each on its own switch. By default it joins the outline only.

- Bold text makes the same halo as regular text, so bold words don't get a heavier backing.

- In light mode, with a wallpaper showing, the halo is drawn lighter than asked, so it looks about as strong as it does in dark mode.

- The default values are a radius of 8 px, a strength of 20%, the SDF function, the exponential falloff, and a 1 px outline.

## Goals

- Text stays readable when the window is fully see-through and the desktop behind it is as light as the text.

- Text stays readable when the window is opaque but the wallpaper is as light as the text.

- The backing looks like one even plate around the text, rather than a separate blob under the letters.

- The scrim costs close to nothing when the text hasn't changed.

### Non-goals

- SilkTerm doesn't turn the scrim off for one program's output. The scrim is one pass over the whole window, and it doesn't know where one command's output starts and ends.

- The scrim doesn't fix text colors. Text that's too close to its background is moved by the minimum contrast floor, which is part of the Themes design.

## Design

### How it is drawn

The scene's text is rendered to a coverage texture, turned into a halo, and composited under the crisp text. It's colored per pixel, so each glyph's backing takes its own cell's background color. A per-pixel background texture is cleared to the global background, and the cell background rectangles are drawn over it. The composite multiplies the halo's coverage by that local color. So a glyph on a colored cell gets a halo that matches its own cell, and that's harmless. Cells on the default background still get their readability halo. A halo isn't drawn over a cell that paints its own solid background.

The outline is drawn in the same composite from the crisp coverage. It dilates the coverage by the outline width, antialiased, and takes its color from the same background map. So it works with the halo off, and then the halo's blur is skipped.

The cursor is a separate coverage texture, so it can join the halo and the outline on independent switches. It's drawn into the scrim's source before the blur, so its halo doesn't cost anything extra. Its animation alpha is applied through the quad color, so a fading or pulsing cursor blurs like glyph coverage. The crisp cursor, and link underlines, are drawn after the scrim is composited. A halo would streak a line that's drawn under it.

Bold would give a heavier halo than regular text. So for a pane with bold in it, a second copy of its text is shaped with the bold taken out, and the scrim is built from that copy. The crisp text keeps its weight. The second shape only costs time on frames that have bold. Where a bold run shapes at a different pitch from regular, as happens on Windows, the bold is kept in the scrim's source, since stripping it would make the halo wider than the text.

The halo is clipped to the terminal area under the chrome, and per pane. At an inside divider it stops at the pane's content edge, and at the window border it stops at the pane edge.

### Halo shape: the scrim function

The halo shape is selectable, because a plain Gaussian blur is a poor legibility backing. It's a round kernel, so as the radius grows the backing rounds off and the corners of a solid block recede. A square of text then looks like it's sitting on a separate round blob rather than an even plate. There are 4 functions, in this order in the dialog:

- **SDF**, the default. The backing grows by true round (Euclidean) distance with full corners. It's round like the old blur, but the corners don't pull in anymore. This is the ideal described below.

- **DT** (distance transform). It uses the same Euclidean distance, but draws it as a solid plate with a crisp feathered edge rather than a soft glow. It looks like a highlighter.

- **Dilate**. The backing grows the same distance from every edge as a square (Chebyshev distance), so corners stay full. It's the most solid and boxy look.

- **Gaussian [ugly]**. It's the old separable blur, and it's kept as a baseline to compare against.

The distance functions share one engine. It's a separable distance transform that's exactly Euclidean and bounded to the halo radius. It takes a 1D distance per column, then combines the rows. That's cheap, at 2 passes and without any jump flood, and either metric can be read off the same field.

#### Why not a Gaussian blur

This is the reasoning the scrim function was built from.

- **What's wrong**: To illustrate conceptually: If you apply a background scrim to a solid square using gaussian blur, as the blur radius increases, the total blur shape looks more and more "round". This means that - effectively - the blur behind the square, doesn't look even at the corners. It looks "too strong" along the middle of the sides of the square, and "pulled-in" at the corners. The corners look naked. Basically it looks like a square sitting on top of a separate round fuzzy thing - rather than something evenly integrated with the square. (Which describes the cursor in block mode perfectly, and also why the scrim behind some clusters of letters looks "clumpy".)

- **What would be better**: Ideally, the blur would also be square-ish - extending evenly from every angle, from every point along the edge of the square. (With corners rounding off with increasing blur radius, but never actually pulling in below the corners.) In other words, if you measured the density fall-off of the blur starting from the corner and moving outward diagonally, it should fall off at about the same rate, as if you measured it from the middle of an edge and moved out perpendicularly.

- **Note**: "Gaussian" isn't just a blur function, it also describes blur falloff. (The Gaussian function makes the bell-shaped normal distribution, the falloff is half of one side.) So while the Gaussian *blur* function is probably the wrong blur to use, the *falloff* model is fine. Whether the two concepts can be separated in practice, is an open question for now, but seems doable (but also there's no reason for it to be a hard requirement - and isn't).
	- As built, they're separate settings. See the falloff below.

- **Solution ideas**:
	- **Distance field blur**. Aka signed distance field blur. This may be the closest match. Compute the signed distance from every pixel to the boundary of the shape, then apply a falloff function (Gaussian, linear, S, etc.) to that distance. Every point one pixel outside the shape has the same opacity regardless of whether it's beside an edge or outside a corner. The corners stay "full" instead of receding.
	- **Morphological dilation followed by feathering**. This might be the easiest and most practical to implement. Common in graphics applications. First expand the shape (using a square or other structuring element). In this case, each character individually on their center (and they'd grow into each other). Then feather the expanded edge - again with a falloff function. This also avoids the rounded-cloud appearance.
	- **Distance transform + transfer function**. Common in vector rendering and font rendering. Rather than convolving with a kernel, opacity is a function of distance from the boundary.
	- **All of them**: Rather than trying to decide which is best in a vacuum, add an item to the config file (and a dropdown selection box in Settings) for "Scrim function", to choose among those three - plus the original "Gaussian [ugly]" (at the bottom). And as long as we're doing that, we might as well add a dropdown selection box for "Scrim falloff".

### The falloff

Separately, a falloff curve shapes how the backing fades with distance. The curves are Exponential, Half-normal, Logarithmic, Sigmoid, and Linear. It's applied both as the blur kernel's weight and as the distance path's transfer. Falloff and function are independent. The function sets the halo's shape, and the falloff sets its fade.

- The falloff is named for the curve it draws rather than for a blur, since the same word otherwise names both a shape and a fade. A bell curve's outer half is a half-normal, and a smoothstep is a sigmoid. The old names "Gaussian" and "S-curve" are still read from a config.

- Every curve is brought to zero at the halo's outer edge, so a halo ends where its radius says it does. Past the edge, the distance passes saturate, and whatever a curve returns there becomes the alpha of every pixel in the pane. The half-normal was once about 1% at its edge, and Strength multiplied that into a wash over the whole pane.

- The exponential curve uses an exponent of 6. A quarter of the way out to the edge it's at 22% of full, and halfway out it's under 5%.

- Past a radius of 20, the halo is kept to the taps it has, so a large radius doesn't flatten it.

### Strength

The third setting is Strength, and it sets how bold the finished halo is. On that scale, each 20% doubles its opacity, up to five doublings at 100%. Because the doubled value is clamped, the halo's core fills first and the solid part grows outward along the falloff. So the backing thickens into a plate rather than only getting brighter, and it still stops at the radius. At 0 the halo is exactly as the function and falloff built it.

The divisor is `SCRIM_PCT_PER_DOUBLING` in `app.rs`. A test checks that this document and `settings_ui.shcl` quote the same number, since they drifted apart once.

The defaults are a radius of 8 px and a strength of 20%. Both were raised when the exponential falloff was made twice as steep, since a curve that drops away sooner has to start further out and heavier to finish in about the same place. The cheaper performance profiles use the same share of the radius, at 5 px, so they still look like the same halo, built with fewer taps. Standard terminal and Remote don't have a halo.

### Light mode

In light mode the halo is a pale plate on the field the picture darkened. The same alpha moves that field much further than it moves dark mode's, for 2 reasons. sRGB's curve is steep near black and flat near white, so dark mode's halo builds slowly and light mode's builds fast. Light mode also draws a picture much further from the paper than dark mode draws it from black, and that's most true of its dark parts.

So light mode redraws every halo alpha, the 1 px outline included, at whatever moves the picture as far toward the background as dark mode's halo moves dark mode's picture. It's measured in sRGB levels, a channel at a time, and averaged over the picture. The composite blends through the pipeline's blend state and can't read what's under it, so the picture's own summary is used instead. That's 16 quantiles of each channel, taken when the wallpaper is prepared. `visibility::halo_match` solves the curve at 12 points, and the shader joins them with straight lines. In dark mode, and in light mode without a wallpaper, the halo is drawn as asked.

These numbers were measured on a screenful of text at the default settings. Each is the mean move per pixel in sRGB levels, against the same scene without any halo or outline, for dark and then light mode. The built-in wallpaper is at 6.6 and 9.5, and light mode was at 20.1 before. A dark photo is at 3.8 and 4.9, from 13.0. A near-black photo is at 2.2 and 2.6, from 13.8. Dark mode's pixels didn't change.

In light mode the halo can also be seen as a pale plate at the edges of letters. That's expected.

### Cost

- The halo is cached on a per-frame signature of the text. A frame whose text didn't change reuses it. The cursor has its own coverage pass, so a blinking cursor doesn't rebuild the halo. That cut the scrim's share of idle cost from 14.7 points to 1.3.

- The outline taps and the cursor sample run only when there is an outline or a cursor scrim to draw.

- The scrim's full-screen textures are made only when needed, and a grid size set from Settings resizes them.

- The 2 blur layers are made only for the halo. Each layer stores only the channels and precision that are read back from it, which is 13 bytes a pixel in all. See "A smaller scrim" in the [reducing resources](20261004-182255_reduce-resources.md#a-smaller-scrim) design doc.

## Alternative ideas

### Unconsidered

- A blur quality setting, with High for no visible artifacts on a strong GPU, Medium for today's quality, and Low for small radii only. Backlog: "At high text scrim blur radius and low softness, the blur has boxy artifacts" (Opened 20260724-080316).

### Rejected

- A Gaussian blur as the only halo. Its round kernel pulls in at the corners of a block. It's kept only as the "ugly" baseline.

- Turning the scrim off per pane for a program that doesn't want it. The request is for that program's output only, and a per-pane switch would also strip the prompt above and the output around it. Backlog: "Config file: For each feature listed below, allow user to list programs" (deferred, Opened 20260708-115155).

### Superseded

- "Text glow", "Glow radius", "Glow border", and `text_glow_*`. They were renamed to text scrim and text outline. Backlog: "Rename "text outer glow" to "text scrim"".

- The scrim was off by default. It's on by default now.

- A Strength scale of 10% per doubling, with a default of 0, then 30. It's 20% per doubling now, with a default of 20. Backlog: "Scrim functions: two falloff curves renamed, and a "Strength" adjustment added" and "Scrim strength: moved to the top of the group".

- S-curve, then Gaussian, then Half-normal as the default falloff. The default is Exponential. Backlog: "Config: scrim and outline renamed, with new defaults" and "Use dropdown list boxes for Scrim function, and Scrim falloff".

- A 2 px outline default, and 2 px in the Low profile. The outline is 1 px everywhere. Backlog: "New default for text outline: 1px" and "Retune the scrim defaults".

- A radius of 5 and a strength of 15. Backlog: "Change defaults" and "Bug: Text sitting under the cursor is hard to read".

- Radio buttons for function and falloff. They're dropdowns now.

- The cursor joining the halo by default. It joins the outline only. Backlog: "Options to include the cursor in the text scrim, and outline".

- A light-mode calibration module (`lightmode.rs`, `PRESENCE`) that scaled the halo and the wallpaper together. The wallpaper half became the visibility model, and the halo half became the gain below. Backlog: "Dark mode is just about perfect, so don't make ANY changes that affect dark mode. But light mode is badly miscalibrated" (closed 20260920).

- One light-mode gain on the halo alpha, matched at half the halo over an average picture, never below a quarter of what was asked, with the outline left at full strength. A single number couldn't follow the curve or the picture, and the outline alone moved pixels by up to 119 levels. The solved curve above replaced it. Backlog: "Light mode: the scrim is much too strong next to dark mode".

## Research findings

- A Gaussian halo on a block of text looks like a round blob under a square, which is also why clusters of letters looked clumpy.

- The separable blur with a truncated kernel leaves a faint edge that low softness makes into a visible square. The linear and sigmoid falloffs aren't true Gaussians, so their support looks like a diamond or a box. Fixing that edge is a trade-off between looks and speed.

- On Windows, bold shapes at a different pitch than regular, so stripping bold for the scrim made the halo wider than the text from the first bold run on.

## Roadmap

- The boxy edge at a high radius and low softness. It needs a pass by eye to balance looks against speed. Backlog: "At high text scrim blur radius and low softness, the blur has boxy artifacts" (Opened 20260724-080316).

- Flip the halo's color under text that had to be lifted for contrast, instead of moving the text color. That would be a fix in the shader rather than the per-cell one that's already built. Backlog: "Flip the scrim color under text that had to be lifted for contrast" (deferred, Opened 20260830-201602).

- A per-program exception for one command's output. It needs each command's output boundaries tracked in the byte stream, mapped onto grid rows every frame, and left out of the coverage source. Don't build it as a per-pane switch. Backlog: "Config file: For each feature listed below, allow user to list programs" (deferred, Opened 20260708-115155).
