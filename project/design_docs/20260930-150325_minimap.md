## Summary

The minimap is a narrow column beside each pane that shows the whole scroll buffer in miniature, like the minimap in Sublime Text or VS Code. Colored regions such as errors, prompts, and diffs can be found from across the room, and the column can be dragged or clicked to move through the buffer. It's on by default.

## Specification

- Each pane has its own map, in a column of its own at the pane's right side. The map never covers the text. Turning it on takes columns from the terminal, like any other resize.

- The column is 100 DIP wide by default, and never takes more than half a pane. A pane that's too narrow to spare the room doesn't get a column.

- The whole buffer, history plus screen, always fits the column top to bottom, oldest first. The map never slides.

- The map shows only as far as the text on screen has reached. Under fast output it doesn't run ahead of the eased view.

- Lines are drawn as colored strokes rather than letters. A busy page looks denser than a sparse one, and a lone colored line keeps its color.

- A marker shows which part of the buffer is on screen. It can be dragged, and a click elsewhere in the column centers the view there. The wheel over the column scrolls the buffer.

- The regular scrollbar is at the far right edge, over the map's edge. Dragging the marker or the scrollbar handle moves both together.

- While a full-screen program runs, the column is hidden and the text takes its width back, unless the program is on a list. The list is empty by default.

- With the map off, it doesn't cost anything. There's no column, no memory, and no work per frame.

- The map must never slow the terminal down noticeably. At the default depth under a flood it costs about 6% of throughput.

- Settings has a switch and a width on the Movement tab, and the View menu has a switch too.

## Goals

- Show the whole buffer at a glance, so the kind of output shows even when the words can't be read.

- Give a quick way to jump anywhere in a deep scrollback.

- Make the map look like text seen from a distance, rather than like bars or blobs.

- Cost nothing when off, and very little when on.

### Non-goals

- Readable text in the map. It's a hint rather than a second view.

- A map of a full-screen program's own content. Such a program draws on a screen that doesn't have a buffer behind it.

## Design

### Where it sits

- There's one map per pane rather than per window. Scrollback belongs to a pane, so in a split each pane has its own map.

- The map takes a real column inside the pane's rectangle, and it never overlays the text. Turning it on costs terminal columns, and the program in the pane is resized as for any other layout change. A pane has 2 rectangles, one for the text and one for the whole pane.

- From left to right, there's the terminal text and then the map. The regular scrollbar is at the pane's far right edge, over the edge of the map, as the same overlay it is without a map. The configured width is the whole column's.

### The mapping

Everything else in the design depends on this mapping.

- The whole buffer, history plus screen, always maps linearly onto the column, top-anchored, oldest first. The editors slide their minimap once the document outgrows it, but this one never does.

- The map stops where the eased text has reached, rather than at the live bottom of the buffer. Under a flood the view trails the newest output by however far the output ease is holding it back, and drawing past that puts lines in the column that aren't on screen. What's kept back is the output the view hasn't come down to yet. New lines add to it as they arrive, and the view uses it up as it reaches them. So a scroll or a jump to the bottom never shortens the column by itself, and typing during a flood doesn't turn the trim off. The extent is set when the picture is composed, so the marker is never measured against a picture that wasn't drawn. A map that came out short asks for another compose and follows the ease down. One line is always kept, so the column never disappears.

- The count of unreached lines is kept inside the scroll model. The scroll position by itself mixes the chase's undrained backlog and a gesture's remaining travel into one number, and only the model can tell them apart.

- The blank rows under a short prompt are part of the buffer, and they take up track.

- With a short buffer, lines draw at a capped height, 1.5 px at 1x, and the map doesn't reach the bottom of the column yet. That cap is scaled but not rounded to whole pixels, on purpose. A line has to be able to start at a fraction of a pixel, or its ink falls inside a single pixel row and a page goes back to looking like a slab.

- With a deep buffer, lines go below a pixel and blend down, so the map compresses instead of scrolling. At the default 10,000-line scrollback a line is a fraction of a pixel. Colored regions still show as bands, which is most of the point.

- The marker is measured at the map's own pitch, over the lines the picture draws rather than over the whole buffer. Anything else puts it above or below the text it stands for whenever the 2 differ, which is every moment the trim is keeping the map short.

- The marker has a floor on its height, so a deep buffer still leaves something to grab. The scrollbar thumb takes the same span rather than its own. Where the floor makes the marker taller than the rows it stands for, it grows both ways from their middle, so it still points at them. Its height and its travel come from one helper, so a drag reads back the same position the marker was drawn at.

### What a line looks like

- Lines are strokes rather than glyphs. Each cell gets a run of its foreground color where there's ink, over the cell's background where that differs from the default. Hues survive, so errors, prompts, and diffs can still be found from across the room.

- How much of its cell a character inks depends on the character, from a quarter for a period or a comma up to the full amount for a hash, a block, or a wide letter. A flat share made a run of text look like one bar. The weights were judged by eye from a monospace face rather than measured, since the map is a hint and the face in use isn't known where the raster runs. A cell with its own background still paints solid whatever is in it. Only how far its color pulls toward the foreground moves.

- Across a line, coverage adds up, so a short or indented line looks like one. Down the column, color is averaged over only the lines that have ink, so a lone red line among blanks keeps its color rather than fading into them.

- A cell is spread over a tent a pixel each side, and so is a line, rather than each being clipped to the pixel it happens to fall in. Neither grid lines up with the pixels. At the ratios a column runs at, about 100 cells into 90 px and about one line per pixel, clipping leaves the 2 grids beating against each other. That draws a comb across the column and broad bands down it, and neither of them is in the text. The wider filter costs about a sixth more per compose, and it makes a page of repeated output look like the text it came from.

- Under about 0.6 px per line the line filter goes back to clipping. A pixel there already averages more than a whole line, the gap between lines is switched off, and the column is even anyway. The wider filter would cost 3 times as much there, on the deep buffer where a compose is already expensive.

- How bright a pixel row gets depends on how much ink fell in it, so a mostly blank stretch looks dimmer than a solid page. That's what makes density readable from a distance. One inked line among many would otherwise almost vanish, so a pixel never falls below a set share of the strongest line in it.

- A line doesn't fill its own height. The gap above and below keeps a page of text from looking like one block. At the capped height the ink is a band narrower than a pixel, so it falls across 2 pixel rows at part strength rather than filling one, which is what a page of text looks like from a distance. Below about half a pixel there isn't room for a gap and the line is taken whole. The 2 are ramped between, so the map doesn't change brightness as a growing buffer crosses that point.

### Full-screen programs

- The column is hidden while a full-screen program runs, and the text gets its width back. Such a program draws on its own screen, which doesn't have a scroll buffer behind it, so the map would show a rectangle at the top of an otherwise empty column.

- A program that redraws itself in place on the main screen counts too. It shows that by asking for mouse reports or focus changes while it runs. A shell turns both off before it runs a command, so a build, a REPL, or `tail -f` keeps the column. Some shells ask for focus changes at their own prompt, so the sign only counts while a command runs. fish does that inside tmux, and a nested fish or an ssh session to fish keeps the column.
	- On Windows the console host asks for focus changes itself as each pane starts, and doesn't pass on a program's own request. So there only the mouse counts.

- While the same program is still running, the column waits a second before it comes back. Some programs turn these modes off and on again as they start, and each change of mind would be a resize they have to redraw for. Back at the prompt, or in another program, it comes back at once.

- Which programs are the exception is a setting, `scroll.minimap.tui_process_whitelist`, rather than a rule. There's no way to tell from the outside whether a full-screen program is one the map could follow usefully, since a pager also runs on its own screen. The list is empty by default. It used to name less, tmux, and screen, but on their own screen the map can only show that screen, which is a rectangle at the top and nothing to scroll.

- The program that counts is the one on screen. In a pipeline that's the last stage still running, so `cat file | less` counts as less, the same as `less file`. The first stage names the process group, but it has often exited by then, and it isn't what draws.

- tmux keeps the column for what it draws itself, and what runs in the pane it shows is judged by the same rule. SilkTerm only sees the tmux client, so it asks tmux which pane the client shows and whether that pane is on its own screen or wants the mouse. It asks once as tmux starts, then again only after the screen changed, at most twice a second, and never waits for the reply. The program in that pane is read from the system with each reply. This works on Linux and macOS.
	- screen doesn't have a plain way to ask the same thing, so a program inside screen keeps the column.
	- tmux over ssh can't be asked at all, and its output alone doesn't show what runs in it. The column goes with ssh, since ssh isn't on the list.
	- A program inside tmux that redraws in place on the main screen and asks only for focus changes keeps the column, since tmux doesn't report that.

- Names match with or without a directory and with or without `.exe`. A process that renames itself, as tmux does with `tmux: client`, matches on the part before the colon.

- Losing the column changes the pane's width, so this is a relayout rather than a drawing choice. There's one on the way in and one on the way out, both where the program repaints anyway.

- For a program on the list, the column stays and the map shows the screen itself, without a marker or a thumb, since there isn't anything to scroll.

### Interaction

- The marker drags like a thumb and follows the scroll target, so it stays exactly under the pointer. A drag is worked out from where it grabbed, rather than from where the marker was last drawn. Because of the height floor, the drawn top is a rounded reading of the position, and reading it back would move the view on a press that never moved.

- The marker and the scrollbar thumb show the same thing, so dragging either one pins both to the pointer. Outside a drag they both follow the eased position and move with the content. When only the dragged one followed the pointer, the other trailed the ease the whole way down and looked like it was lagging.

- A handle that's let go stays where it was dropped until the text arrives, rather than being handed straight back to the eased position. The text is still on its way at that moment, so handing it back sent the handle backward the way it came and then crawling forward again. That's a bounce, on the one gesture where the user has said exactly where they want to be. The wait ends when the 2 agree, or at once if anything else moves the target, since from there the handle belongs to the content again.

- A click elsewhere in the column centers the view there, eased the same way a scrollbar drag settles. The bottom of the map stands for the lines the trim is keeping back as well as the last one it drew, so a click there means the newest output.

- The wheel over the column scrolls the buffer, the same as over the text. That's true even under a program that's tracking the mouse, since there isn't a cell under the pointer to report.

### Settings and chrome

- The Movement tab has a "Minimap" switch and a width slider under the scrollbar, and the View menu has an item for it too.

- The marker reuses the scrollbar's thumb color, which is why the scrollbar color rows are with the palette on the Themes tab rather than under the scrollbar switch.

### How it is built

- `minimap.rs` has the line cache, the raster, the mapping, and the hit tests. `pane.rs` carves out the rectangle and routes events. Drawing is one textured quad per pane, plus overlay quads for the marker and the thumb.

- Each line is rasterized once into a fixed-width pixel row, at the first compose after it enters history, since history lines never change. The live screen rows are rasterized again at each compose. A build between composes only counts the new lines. It runs with the lock held that the program's reader waits on, and under a flood most lines leave history before any compose would show them, so rasterizing them as they arrived cost about half the terminal's speed.

- A screen swap, a resize, and a width change drop the cache. Sitting scrolled back with a full scrollback is the one case where nothing reports how many lines were pushed. So a changed newest history line is taken as the sign that the cache has fallen behind, and the cache is rebuilt whole at a limited rate.

- A compose reads at most about 4 ms worth of lines from the grid. A deep scrollback that was just rebuilt, or turned over by a flood, has far more than that. Reading 100,000 lines took 150 ms, and the terminal froze for all of it. Past the limit, one line in every so many is drawn and stands in for the lines around it. Later composes spend what's left of the limit replacing the stand-ins, newest first, since the oldest are the first to leave. Once output stops, the map ends up the same as one drawn from every line. Until then a pixel row can show a neighbor's picture of a line, and under a flood nobody can tell.

- A compose that only replaced stand-ins redoes just the pixel rows they fall in, and the screen's rows. Each pixel row is worked out on its own, so that matches a whole compose.

- Redrawing the whole image is pixel work on the cached rows, and it doesn't need a lock. Over a deep scrollback it's still most of a second at a million lines, so the rows are sent to a thread of their own for it, and the finished image is swapped in when they come back. The builds keep counting meanwhile. A small redraw is cheaper to do in place.

- The composed image is uploaded as a texture the size of the column, so texture size limits and the GL context's re-upload after a VRAM loss aren't a problem.

- Under a flood every pixel of the map moves on every line, so a recompose is throttled rather than run per frame. A compose that the throttle defers schedules a timed wake rather than setting an animation flag. Marking the window as animating would bring it straight back, find the throttle still closed, and spin at the frame rate. A compose can leave the next one pending, and that wake is only known once its frame is drawn, so the event loop looks for it again after drawing.

- A short map leaves another compose pending only while the unreached count is draining. A view parked in the scrollback freezes that count, and leaving one pending for as long as the map was short asked for a whole redraw about 11 times a second without any output.

- The throttle is at least 90 ms, and at least 20 times what the last ordinary compose took, the part on its own thread included. A whole redraw after a screen swap, a resize, or a resync costs far more, and waiting 20 times that froze the map for seconds, so a whole redraw doesn't count. A column that changes size composes at once instead of waiting out the throttle, so the image is never left at a size the column doesn't have anymore. A whole compose still grows with the scrollback, which can be a million lines, so a fixed interval couldn't limit its share at every depth.

- Memory is about 5 MB per pane at the default scrollback and a 120 px column, and it's freed while the map is off.

- When the map is off, there's no column, no cache, and no per-frame work. The whole feature depends on one config check, and the cache is freed the moment the column goes away.

## Alternative ideas

### Rejected

- A second, always-visible scrollbar between the text and the map, to show that bar is for the terminal. The 2 showed the same thing at the same size. Backlog: "Remove the double-scrollbar with the minimap".

- Noise or jitter to break up the moire. It trades a pattern for grain, and has to be stable across composes or the column shimmers. Backlog: "The minimap looks MUCH better, but shows aliasing or moire artifacts".

- A heavier resampling kernel over the whole buffer, and the wider line filter below 0.6 px per line. Both cost in proportion on a deep buffer for no visible gain. Same backlog item.

- Trimming the map to the last screen row with output. The blank rows under a prompt are part of the buffer.

- Trimming whenever the view follows the bottom, and trimming only while the output chase controls the motion. The first took a jump to the bottom as output, and the second lost the rest of a flood after one keystroke. Backlog: "Minimap: stop the map where the eased text has reached".

- Rasterizing each line as it enters history. Under a flood it cost about half the terminal's speed.

- Telling pagers from editors automatically. A pager runs on its own screen too, so nothing tells them apart. The list names the exceptions. Backlog: "Auto-disable the minimap when in a TUI".

### Superseded

- Off by default. It's been on by default since 20260917, once the column could hide itself for full-screen programs. Backlog: "Minimap on by default".

- Keeping the strongest ink when many lines blend into one pixel row. A pixel row is now as bright as the ink that fell in it, with a floor so one inked line still shows. Backlog: ""Minimap" feature" and "Minimap: Unless a line of text is rendering below 1px".

- Lines 2 pixels tall with the ink filling one of them, and one strength for every inked cell. Lines are 1.5 px with a band of ink under a pixel, and each character has its own weight. Backlog: "Minimap:" (closed 20260919-165500).

- The column staying on for every full-screen program.

- A marker measured against the whole buffer. It's measured at the map's own pitch now. Backlog: "Minimap: the marker sits above the part of the map the screen is showing".

## Research findings

- With the map drawing every line as it arrived, the plain speed row read 36.5 MB/s against 67.8 with the map off. After the fix, 32 MiB of plain text ran at 53.0 MiB/s with the map on against 56.9 off. At a 100,000-line scrollback it was 36.5 against 42.2, up from 13.4.

- At 100,000 lines a redraw stalled the terminal 154 to 164 ms, and at a million lines about 2 s. With the read limit and stand-ins it is 20 to 24 ms, and under 10 ms. Filling the stand-ins in takes from seconds to about a minute.

- One slow 445 ms whole redraw once led to an 8.95 s wait before the next.

- A compose over 20,000 lines at 200 columns costs about 30 to 40 ms, and about 170 to 200 ms at 100,000.

- The tent filter took a page of identical lines from 18% variation down the column to 2%.

- Under a flood of 600 lines a second, the marker and the map differed by 218 against 225 lines before the marker moved to the map's pitch.
