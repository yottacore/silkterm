## Summary

The minimap is a narrow column beside each pane that shows the whole scroll buffer in miniature, like the minimap in Sublime Text or VS Code. Colored regions such as errors, prompts and diffs can be found from across the room, and the column can be dragged or clicked to move through the buffer. It is on by default.

## Specification

- Each pane has its own map, in a column of its own at the pane's right side. The map never covers the text. Turning it on takes columns from the terminal, like any other resize.

- The column is 100 DIP wide by default, and never takes more than half a pane. A pane too narrow to spare the room gets no column.

- The whole buffer, history plus screen, always fits the column top to bottom, oldest first. The map never slides.

- The map shows only as far as the text on screen has reached. Under fast output it does not run ahead of the eased view.

- Lines are drawn as colored strokes, not letters. A busy page looks denser than a sparse one, and a lone colored line keeps its color.

- A marker shows which part of the buffer is on screen. It can be dragged, and a click elsewhere in the column centers the view there. The wheel over the column scrolls the buffer.

- The regular scrollbar sits at the far right edge, over the map's edge. Dragging the marker or the scrollbar handle moves both together.

- While a full-screen program runs, the column steps aside and the text takes its width back, unless the program is on a list. The list is less, tmux and screen by default.

- With the map off, it costs nothing: no column, no memory and no work per frame.

- The map must never slow the terminal down noticeably. At the default depth under a flood it costs about 6% of throughput.

- Settings has a switch and a width on the Movement tab, and the View menu has a switch too.

## Goals

- A picture of the whole buffer at a glance, where the kind of output shows even when the words cannot be read.

- A quick way to jump anywhere in a deep scrollback.

- A map that reads like text seen from a distance, not like bars or blobs.

- No cost when off, and very little when on.

### Non-goals

- Readable text in the map. It is a hint, not a second view.

- A map of a full-screen program's own content. Such a program draws on a screen with no buffer behind it.

## Design

### Where it sits

- Per pane, not per window. Scrollback belongs to a pane, so in a split each pane carries its own map.

- The map owns a real column inside the pane's rectangle. It never overlays the text. Turning it on costs terminal columns, and the program in the pane is resized like for any other layout change. A pane carries two rectangles: the text's and the whole pane's.

- Left to right: terminal text, then the map. The regular scrollbar sits at the pane's far right edge, over the edge of the map, the same overlay it is without a map. The configured width is the whole column's.

### The mapping

This is the decision everything else rests on.

- The whole buffer, history plus screen, always maps linearly onto the column, top-anchored, oldest first. The editors slide their minimap once the document outgrows it. This one never does.

- The map stops where the eased text has reached, not at the live bottom of the buffer. Under a flood the view sits behind the newest output by however far the output ease is keeping it, and drawing past that puts lines in the column that are not on screen. What is kept back is the output the view has not come down to yet. New lines add to it as they arrive, and the view gives it back as it reaches them. So a scroll or a jump to the bottom never shortens the column by itself, and typing during a flood does not turn the trim off. The extent is settled when the picture is composed, so the marker is never measured against a picture nobody drew. A map that came out short asks for another compose and follows the ease down. One line is always kept, so the column never disappears.

- The count of unreached lines lives inside the scroll model. The scroll position alone carries the chase's undrained backlog and a gesture's remaining travel in one number, and only the model can tell them apart.

- The blank rows under a short prompt are part of the buffer, and take track.

- With a short buffer, lines draw at a capped height, 1.5 px at 1x, and the map just does not reach the bottom of the column yet. That cap is scaled but not rounded to whole pixels, on purpose. A line has to be able to sit at a fraction of one, or its ink falls inside a single pixel row and a page goes back to reading as a slab.

- With a deep buffer, lines go below a pixel and blend down, so the map compresses instead of scrolling. At the default 10,000-line scrollback a line is a fraction of a pixel. Colored regions still read as bands, which is most of the point.

- The marker is measured at the map's own pitch, over the lines the picture draws rather than over the whole buffer. Anything else puts it above or below the text it stands for whenever the two differ, which is every moment the trim is keeping the map short.

- The marker has a floor on its height, so a deep buffer still leaves something to grab. The scrollbar thumb takes the same span, never its own. Where the floor makes the marker taller than the rows it stands for, it grows both ways from their middle, so it still points at them. Its height and its travel come from one helper, so a drag reads back the same position the marker was drawn at.

### What a line looks like

- Strokes, not glyphs. Per cell: a run of the cell's foreground color where there is ink, over the cell's background where it differs from the default. Hues survive, so errors, prompts and diffs stay findable from across the room.

- How much of its cell a character inks varies with the character, from a quarter for a period or a comma up to the full amount for a hash, a block or a wide letter. A flat share is what made a run of text read as one bar. The weights are judged by eye from a monospace face rather than measured, since the map is a hint and the face in use is not known where the raster runs. A cell with its own background still paints solid whatever is in it. Only how far its color pulls toward the foreground moves.

- Across a line, coverage adds up, so a short or indented line reads as one. Down the column, color is averaged over only the lines that have ink, so a lone red line among blanks keeps its color rather than fading into them.

- A cell is spread over a tent a pixel each side, and so is a line, rather than each being clipped to the pixel it happens to fall in. Neither grid lines up with the pixels. At the ratios a column runs at, about 100 cells into 90 px and about one line per pixel, clipping leaves the two grids beating against each other. That draws a comb across the column and broad bands down it, neither of which is in the text. The wider filter costs about a sixth more per compose, and it is what makes a page of repeated output read as the text it came from.

- Under about 0.6 px per line the line filter goes back to clipping. A pixel there already averages more than a whole line, the gap between lines is switched off and the column is even anyway. The wider filter would cost three times as much there, on the deep buffer where a compose is already the expensive one.

- How bright a pixel row gets is how much ink actually fell in it, so a mostly blank stretch reads dimmer than a solid page. That is what makes density readable from a distance. One inked line among many would otherwise almost vanish, so a pixel never falls below a set share of the strongest line in it.

- A line does not fill its own height. The gap above and below is what stops a page of text reading as one block. At the capped height the ink is a band narrower than a pixel, so it falls across two pixel rows at part strength rather than filling one, which is what a page of text looks like from a distance. Below about half a pixel there is no room for a gap and the line is taken whole, with the two ramped between so the map does not change brightness as a growing buffer crosses that point.

### Full-screen programs

- The column steps aside while a full-screen program runs, and the text gets its width back. Such a program draws on its own screen, which has no scroll buffer behind it, so the map would show a rectangle at the top of an otherwise empty column.

- Which programs are the exception is a setting, `scroll.minimap.tui_process_whitelist`, rather than a rule. There is no way to tell from the outside whether a full-screen program is one the map could usefully follow, since a pager also runs on its own screen. By default the list names a pager and the two multiplexers.

- Names match with or without a directory and with or without `.exe`. A process that renames itself, as tmux does with `tmux: client`, matches on the part before the colon.

- Losing the column changes the pane's width, so this is a relayout rather than a drawing choice: one on the way in and one on the way out, both where the program repaints anyway.

- For a program on the list, the column stays and the map shows the screen itself, with no marker and no thumb, since there is nothing to scroll.

### Interaction

- The marker drags like a thumb and rides the scroll target, so it follows the pointer exactly. A drag works out from where it grabbed, rather than from where the marker was last drawn. The height floor means the drawn top is a rounded reading of the position, and reading it back would move the view on a press that never moved.

- The marker and the scrollbar thumb say the same thing, so dragging either one pins both to the pointer. Off a drag they both ride the eased position and move with the content. Letting only the dragged one ride the pointer left the other trailing the ease the whole way down, which read as the second one lagging.

- A handle let go stays where it was dropped until the text arrives, rather than being handed straight back to the eased position. The text is still on its way at that moment, so handing it back sent the handle backward the way it came and then crawling forward again. That is a bounce, on the one gesture where the user has said exactly where they want to be. The wait ends when the two agree, or at once if anything else moves the target, since from there the handle belongs to the content again.

- A click elsewhere in the column centers the view there, eased the same way a scrollbar drag settles. The bottom of the map stands for the lines the trim is keeping back as well as the last one it drew, so a click there means the newest output.

- The wheel over the column scrolls the buffer, same as over the text. That is true even under a program that is tracking the mouse, since there is no cell under the pointer to report.

### Settings and chrome

- A "Minimap" switch and a width slider on the Movement tab, under the scrollbar, plus a View-menu item.

- The marker reuses the scrollbar's thumb color, which is why the scrollbar color rows sit with the palette on the Themes tab rather than under the scrollbar switch.

### How it is built

- `minimap.rs` owns the line cache, the raster, the mapping and the hit tests. `pane.rs` carves the rectangle and routes events. Drawing is one textured quad per pane, plus overlay quads for the marker and the thumb.

- Each line rasterizes once into a fixed-width pixel row, at the first compose after it enters history, since history lines never change. The live screen rows raster again at each compose. A build between composes only counts the new lines. It runs while holding the lock the program's reader waits on, and under a flood most lines leave history before any compose would show them, so rasterizing them as they arrived cost about half the terminal's speed.

- A screen swap, a resize and a width change drop the cache. Sitting scrolled back with a full scrollback is the one case where nothing reports how many lines were pushed. So a changed newest history line is taken as the sign the cache has fallen behind, and it rebuilds whole at a bounded rate.

- A compose reads at most about 4 ms worth of lines from the grid. A deep scrollback that was just rebuilt, or turned over by a flood, has far more than that. Reading 100,000 lines took 150 ms, and the terminal stood still for all of it. Past the limit, one line in every so many is drawn and stands in for the lines around it. Later composes spend what is left of the limit replacing the stand-ins, newest first, since the oldest are the first to leave. Once output stops, the map ends up the one every line makes. Until then a pixel row can show a neighbor's picture of a line, which under a flood nobody can tell.

- A compose that only replaced stand-ins redoes just the pixel rows they fall in, and the screen's. Each pixel row is worked out on its own, so that matches a whole compose.

- Redrawing the whole image is pixel work on the cached rows, and it needs no lock. Over a deep scrollback it is still most of a second at a million lines, so the rows go to a thread of their own for it, and the finished image is swapped in when they come back. The builds carry on counting meanwhile. A small one is cheaper to do in place.

- The composed image uploads as a texture the size of the column, so texture size limits and the GL context's re-upload after a VRAM loss are not a problem.

- Under a flood every pixel of the map moves on every line, so a recompose is throttled rather than run per frame. A compose the throttle defers schedules a timed wake, not an animation flag. Marking the window animating would bring it straight back, find the throttle still closed, and spin at the frame rate. A compose can owe the next one, and that wake is only known once its frame is drawn, so the event loop looks for it again after drawing.

- A short map owes another compose only while the unreached count is draining. A view parked in the scrollback freezes that count, and owing one for as long as it was short asked for a whole redraw about eleven times a second with no output at all.

- The throttle is at least 90 ms, and at least twenty times what the last ordinary compose took, the part on its own thread included. A whole redraw after a screen swap, a resize or a resync costs far more, and waiting twenty times that left the map still for seconds, so it does not count. A column that changes size composes at once instead of waiting the throttle out, so the image is never left at a size the column no longer has. A whole compose still grows with the scrollback, which can be a million lines, so a fixed interval could not bound its share at every depth.

- Memory is about 5 MB per pane at the default scrollback and a 120 px column, freed while the map is off.

- Truly off: no column, no cache and no per-frame work. The whole feature hangs off one config check, and the cache is freed the moment the column goes away.

## Alternative ideas

### Rejected

- A second, always-visible scrollbar beside the map, between the text and the map, to say that bar is for the terminal. The two showed the same thing at the same size. Backlog: "Remove the double-scrollbar with the minimap".

- Noise or jitter to break up the moire. It trades a pattern for grain, and has to be stable across composes or the column shimmers. Backlog: "The minimap looks MUCH better, but shows aliasing or moire artifacts".

- A heavier resampling kernel over the whole buffer, and the wider line filter below 0.6 px per line. Both cost in proportion on a deep buffer for no visible gain. Same backlog item.

- Trimming the map to the last screen row with output. The blank rows under a prompt are part of the buffer.

- Trimming whenever the view follows the bottom, and trimming only while the output chase owns the motion. The first read a jump to the bottom as output, and the second lost the rest of a flood after one keystroke. Backlog: "Minimap: stop the map where the eased text has reached".

- Rasterizing each line as it enters history. Under a flood it cost about half the terminal's speed.

- Telling pagers from editors automatically. A pager runs on its own screen too, so nothing tells them apart. The list names the exceptions. Backlog: "Auto-disable the minimap when in a TUI".

### Superseded

- Off by default. It is on by default since 20260917, once the column learned to step aside for full-screen programs. Backlog: "Minimap on by default".

- Keeping the strongest ink when many lines blend into one pixel row. A pixel row is now as bright as the ink that fell in it, with a floor so one inked line still shows. Backlog: ""Minimap" feature" and "Minimap: Unless a line of text is rendering below 1px".

- Two-pixel lines with the ink filling one of them, and one strength for every inked cell. Lines are 1.5 px with a band of ink under a pixel, and each character has its own weight. Backlog: "Minimap:" (closed 20260919-165500).

- The column staying on for every full-screen program.

- A marker measured against the whole buffer. It is measured at the map's own pitch. Backlog: "Minimap: the marker sits above the part of the map the screen is showing".

## Research findings

- With the map drawing every line as it arrived, the plain speed row read 36.5 MB/s against 67.8 with the map off. After the fix, 32 MiB of plain text ran at 53.0 MiB/s with the map on against 56.9 off. At a 100,000-line scrollback it was 36.5 against 42.2, up from 13.4.

- At 100,000 lines a redraw stalled the terminal 154 to 164 ms, and at a million lines about 2 s. With the read limit and stand-ins it is 20 to 24 ms, and under 10 ms. Filling the stand-ins in takes from seconds to about a minute.

- One slow 445 ms whole redraw once led to an 8.95 s wait before the next.

- A compose over 20,000 lines at 200 columns costs about 30 to 40 ms, and about 170 to 200 ms at 100,000.

- The tent filter took a page of identical lines from 18% variation down the column to 2%.

- Under a 600 lines a second flood, the marker and the map differed by 218 against 225 lines before the marker moved to the map's pitch.

## Roadmap

- A look at the map under a real flood. The trim is pinned by numbers, not by eye.

- The published speed rows want a proper run with the map on, since it is on by default.

- The map with screen has not been tried.
